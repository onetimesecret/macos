Loro Concepts, Applied

The fifteen concepts in Loro's documentation, read closely, then turned toward how people actually behave inside writing applications.

All fifteen pages under loro.dev/docs → Concepts were read on 29 Aug 2026, plus the Undo/Redo and Document Size pages for numbers the concepts lean on. The writing-behavior grounding comes from this project's earlier survey of version history across ~100 products ("Undo That Survives Closing," same date). Nothing here is invented; where a figure could not be re-verified it is marked unverified.

How to read "classic, conventional, novel"

Classic is the distributed-systems use the concept was born for — what a CRDT textbook or sync-server author would do with it. Conventional is how a mainstream writing product (a Google-Docs-, Notion-, or iA-Writer-shaped app) would employ it today. Novel is a use the concept supports but few or no surveyed products ship — each one anchored to an observed behavior or documented pain from real writing tools, not to speculation about users.

The grounding: how people actually use writing apps

Five patterns from the incumbent survey do most of the work in this report.

Writing is mostly a party of one. The bulk of surveyed writing tools are single-user apps with sync (Obsidian, Ulysses, Drafts, Scrivener, iA Writer…), not co-editors. The commonest "collaboration" is a person with two devices — and that is exactly where things break: conflicted-copy dialogs (Standard Notes ships a conflict diff), lowest-device-wins retention (Joplin), and cross-device draft reverts (a recurring Gmail complaint thread). The concurrency machinery below matters to solo writers first.

History is hand-rolled at session granularity. Where incumbents keep versions at all, they build timers: Standard Notes ≥5 min apart, Joplin and HackMD every 10 min, Word AutoRecover 10 min, Dropbox Paper "≥30 min old and >500 chars changed," Figma 30-min checkpoints. People evidently think in sittings, not keystrokes.

Restore is feared because it destroys. Dropbox Paper's rollback is destructive; Cursor's checkpoint restore wipes redo; Zed had an undo-wipes-restored-buffer bug. Several apps only dare offer restore-as-copy (Joplin, Reflect, Mem's append-on-restore).

Undo dies at the window. Apple Notes, Bear, Heynote, and every major email client ship no draft history at all; closing the window is the cliff. This was the largest single gap the earlier outing found.

Provenance is becoming a feature. iA Writer's Authorship annotations and Linear's per-span author attribution (Jul 2026) mark a shift: readers of a draft increasingly want to know who — or what — wrote each span.

Findings

F1

Loro's architecture already contains, as substrate, the entire feature set incumbents hand-roll on top. OpLog + checkout + frontiers is version history, named versions, preview-before-restore, and a diff base in one mechanism — the features the surveyed apps build with timers, copy files, and SQLite sidecars.

F2

The economics of "keep everything" now work. On the standard Automerge-paper trace (259,778 single-character ops → 104,852-char document), Loro's full history exports at 251 KB raw / 106 KB compressed — about 0.97 and 0.41 bytes per keystroke-level operation. Full history costs roughly the same as the final text itself.

F3

Change merging natively produces the "session" units apps build by hand. Consecutive same-peer ops merge into one Change, split by imports, differing commit messages, or a timestamp gap (docs state a 1000-second default window when timestamps are on) — nearly the same shape as Standard Notes' ≥5-min and Joplin's 10-min hand-rolled sessions.

F4

Undo is local-by-design, and that is the collaborative-UX best practice. UndoManager (default 100 steps, 1000 ms merge) reverts only the current peer's operations, with cursor save/restore hooks — the semantics ProseMirror and Quill converged on, here at the document layer.

F5

Shallow snapshots give writing apps a capability almost none of the ~100 surveyed products has: truly deleting something from history. Delete the sensitive text, export shallow-snapshot, and prior operations are permanently gone ("typically 70–90% smaller"; the benchmark shows −76.8%).

F6

Two of the fifteen concepts are warnings, not features. "How to Choose the Right CRDT Types" and "When Not to Rely on CRDTs" both say the same thing from different sides: merge semantics are a product decision per field, and anything needing global agreement (locks, uniqueness, publishing) still needs an authority.

F7

Named versions become effectively free. A Frontier is 1–2 operation IDs regardless of collaborator count, so bookmarking a version at every export, send, or long pause costs bytes — where incumbents ration named versions (HackMD: 10 on the free plan).

Concepts 1–2

Foundations

01What are CRDTs

Replicated data structures whose replicas can be updated independently and in parallel, converging without coordination. Loro's page frames it as the CAP trade: availability + partition-tolerance + strong eventual consistency, at the cost of larger documents and harder intent-preservation than OT.

Classic

Real-time co-editing and peer-to-peer sync without a central server — the Google-Docs-competitor scenario the literature was written for.

Conventional

What Anytype already does: a notes app whose sync layer is CRDT-based, so multi-device edits merge instead of forking. Collaboration cursors and shared workspaces sit on top.

Novel

Treat yourself on two devices as the primary concurrency case. The survey's real pain was never "two authors, one sentence" — it was conflicted copies, lowest-device-wins version pruning, and cross-device draft reverts. A writing app can adopt CRDTs purely to make the phone-edit-on-the-train merge silently into the laptop draft, with co-editing as a side effect it may never enable.

02Container

Typed building blocks — Map, List, MovableList, Text, Tree, Counter — addressed by deterministic ContainerIDs. Concurrent setContainer() at the same key keeps only one winner; the ensureMergeable* methods derive identity from logical position so peers converge on one shared child instead.

Classic

Model app state: a root Map of document metadata holding a Text body, a List of comments, a Tree for folder structure.

Conventional

Block-based documents à la Notion: each block a container, giving per-block merge behavior and per-block events, with the page as a list of block containers.

Novel

Date-keyed ensureMergeableText("2026-08-29") for daily-notes apps: two offline devices both "creating today's note" converge to one note instead of duplicates (a failure mode the daily-note pattern in Roam/Logseq-style tools invites; scenario, not a cited bug report). Same trick for per-scene containers in a Scrivener-style manuscript — history, merge, and restore then operate at the scene level writers actually think in.

Concepts 3–6

State and history

03Attached vs Detached States

Two orthogonal ideas share the words: a document is detached when checkout(frontiers) points its state at a historical version (editing disabled by default; attach() returns to latest), and a container is detached when built standalone (new LoroMap()) before insertion gives back an attached copy.

Classic

Time travel: check out any historical frontier for inspection, then re-attach — version control semantics inside the document.

Conventional

A read-only version-preview pane, Google-Docs style: the history view is a detached checkout, so "look at Tuesday's version" never risks the live document.

Novel

Fix the restore problem the survey kept finding (Dropbox Paper's destructive rollback; Cursor's restore-destroys-redo). Because checkout is non-destructive and the OpLog keeps everything, "restore" can be recast as preview, then either copy text forward or apply a reverse diff as new operations — the old version and the redo path both survive. Detached is also the natural read-mode for a Simplenote-style scrub slider.

04OpLog and DocState Separation

The append-only history (OpLog) and the materialized current document (DocState) are architecturally separate, each with its own version pointer. Export modes span the split: update (history only), snapshot (both), shallow-snapshot (state plus trimmed history).

Classic

Relay/sync servers hold and forward the OpLog without ever materializing the document; clients load a snapshot fast and fetch history lazily.

Conventional

Fast app launch from a snapshot, with the full revision timeline streamed in when the user opens the history panel — the Notion/Google-Docs loading pattern without the server doing bespoke work.

Novel

This separation is the answer to the earlier outing's title problem, "undo that survives closing." Every zero-history app surveyed (Apple Notes, Bear, Heynote, Gmail/Outlook drafts) conflates state with history, so closing the buffer discards the past. With an OpLog, keystroke-level history is a property of the storage format — and Draftback-style writing replay, pause analytics, and "what did I cut yesterday" queries become reads over data the app already has.

05Operations and Changes

Operations are atomic edits identified by (peerId, counter); Changes are batches of consecutive same-peer operations plus metadata (timestamp, dependencies, optional commit message). Merging is automatic and splits on imports, differing commit messages, or timestamp gaps — the docs give a 1000-second default window when timestamps are enabled.

Classic

Compression of sync metadata: a burst of typing travels as one Change, not thousands of individually-stamped ops.

Conventional

Change boundaries as the revision-list entries in a history UI, with commit messages as edit summaries — the Wikipedia/Git idiom applied to a document.

Novel

The survey showed every incumbent hand-rolling "session" granularity with timers (≥5 min, 10 min, 30 min). Change-merge windows produce those units natively — and writing-process research (Wengelin; Van Waes & Leijten) finds that pauses mark cognitive boundaries. Committing on pause, with the pause length tuned like a debounce, would make history units coincide with units of thought rather than units of wall-clock.

06Transaction Model

Explicitly not ACID: a Loro "transaction" is an event-emission and grouping boundary. Pending local ops emit one event and form one Change at commit(); import/export/checkout commit implicitly. No rollback exists.

Classic

Batch a compound edit (set title + insert body + tag) into one event so observers re-render once, coherently.

Conventional

One transaction per user gesture — paste, drag, find-and-replace — so the events feeding the UI and the sync layer match what the user did.

Novel

Transaction boundaries are undo boundaries. When an AI assistant rewrites three paragraphs across two containers, wrapping it in one commit (plus UndoManager's groupStart/groupEnd) makes "reject the suggestion" a single gesture — the exact ergonomics AI-writing tools currently fumble. The caveat cuts the other way: with no rollback, "cancel this draft edit" must be built from checkout or reverse ops, not from the transaction model.

Concepts 7–8

Naming versions

07Frontiers

A version named by its leaf operations — the ops with no successors — implicitly including all ancestors. Typically 1–2 elements regardless of peer count. Best where the history is at hand (checkout targets, checkpoints); convertible via frontiersToVV() / vvToFrontiers().

Classic

Compact checkout targets and DAG bookmarks — the commit-hash of the CRDT world.

Conventional

Named versions à la Scrivener snapshots or HackMD: store a frontier plus a label, jump back on demand. At a few bytes each, no plan-gated rationing needed.

Novel

Auto-bookmark the moments writers actually return to: every export, print, paste-into-email, or send-to-editor stamps a frontier ("the version I submitted"). None of the ~40 writing tools surveyed versions on share — yet "what did I send them?" is the question a version list is opened to answer. absence verified only across the surveyed set

08Version Vector

A map from peerID to operation count — self-contained, comparable, O(peers) in size. The synchronization counterpart to Frontiers: it can answer "what are you missing?" without access to the other side's history.

Classic

Delta sync negotiation: exchange vectors, send exactly the missing ops.

Conventional

Efficient multi-device sync in a notes app — each device pulls precisely the operations it lacks, over any transport.

Novel

Surface it. The cross-device pains in the survey (Gmail draft reverts, Joplin's lowest-device-wins) are invisible-divergence problems. A status line computed from version vectors — "phone is 132 edits behind; last merged 2h ago" — turns sync from an article of faith into something inspectable, which is what backup-anxious writers keep asking forums about.

Concepts 9–11

Positions, delivery, identity

09Cursor and Stable Positions

Positions anchored to operation IDs rather than indices (getCursor(index, side) / getCursorPos(), with Before/Middle/After sides), so they stay meaningful as concurrent edits move the text around them.

Classic

Live collaborator carets and selections that don't jump when someone else types above them.

Conventional

Comment and annotation anchoring that survives edits — the thing Google Docs comments do well and most Markdown-file-based tools cannot do at all.

Novel

Solo uses incumbents lack: a "where I left off" position that syncs across devices; TODO/⚠ markers pinned inside a draft that survive heavy revision; AI suggestion anchors that stay attached to the right sentence while the human keeps typing above. Also the docs' own subtle one: saving cursors in UndoManager's onPush so undo restores the selection too — polish that distinguishes editors people trust.

10Import Status and Pending Operations

import() returns which operation ranges applied and which are pending on missing causal dependencies; pending ops apply automatically once their prerequisites arrive. Silent incompleteness is possible if the status is never checked.

Classic

Correctness under out-of-order, at-least-once message delivery — the reality of every real network transport.

Conventional

Robust sync over flaky links: mobile radios, background tabs, resumed laptops. Batch import for catching up after a week offline.

Novel

Transport-agnostic writing sync: because updates are just bytes that can arrive in any order, a draft can sync via email attachment, AirDrop, a USB stick, or a static file host — no vendor sync service. For the local-first, files-on-disk audience (Obsidian's constituency), that is the difference between "our cloud" and "your channel." The pending set doubles as an honest UI: "waiting on 1 update from your laptop."

11PeerID Management

Every editing session gets a 64-bit peerID; ops are (peerId, counter). Fresh random IDs per session are the safe default; the page's sharpest warnings are against reuse — never a user ID (users have multiple devices), and reuse only with persisted local state loaded first.

Classic

Globally unique operation identity without coordination — the substrate everything above sits on.

Conventional

Session identity mapped (externally) to accounts for presence and audit — "Dana is editing" — while the CRDT layer keeps its own IDs.

Novel

Give the AI assistant its own peer. Every AI-written span is then attributable at the operation level for free — the iA Writer Authorship / Linear per-span-attribution feature, derived rather than built, and ready for provenance-disclosure demands. Per-device peers likewise make "written on my phone" a filterable dimension of history. no surveyed product ships op-level AI attribution

Concepts 12–13

The engine and its economics

12Event Graph Walker (Eg-Walker)

Instead of storing heavy CRDT metadata per op, record plain index-based edits and replay the concurrent slice of the DAG when merging. Local edits stay O(1)/O(log n); replay cost is bounded by concurrency, not document size; fully-synced tombstones become garbage-collectable. Loro is "heavily inspired by" Eg-Walker atop Fugue, not a strict implementation.

Classic

Make long-lived documents viable: keep years of history without the per-op metadata tax that sank earlier CRDTs.

Conventional

Invisible speed — instant open, small files, cheap merges. Users never see the algorithm; they see that history-everything doesn't slow the app down.

Novel

Replay as a product surface. The merge already computes how two branches interleave — expose it, and a fork of chapter 3 edited for two weeks merges back with a track-changes-style account of what interleaved where. Long-lived forks are exactly what Scrivener-class writers simulate today with "Draft 2 (copy) FINAL-v3.docx" files.

13Shallow Snapshots and Redaction

Git-shallow-clone for documents: export current state with history trimmed at a frontier — "typically 70–90% smaller" — and, by deleting sensitive text first, make it permanently absent from the exported history. Hard limit: peers can only sync from versions after the trim point.

Classic

Storage GC and fast cold-start for documents with years of operations.

Conventional

Retention policy as an export policy: Notion's 7/30/90/unlimited-day history tiers, or Joplin's 90-day default, become "how far back do we shallow-trim," implemented in one mechanism per plan.

Novel

True deletion — near-absent across the surveyed set, where history either doesn't exist or can't forget. Three grounded shapes: journaling apps that honor "I need this gone" (and GDPR erasure) into history, not just current text; "share a clean copy" that hands a collaborator the document without your drafting process (the discomfort Draftback exposed about keystroke replay); unpublishing a pasted secret from a shared doc's history. The sync-boundary caveat means this is a deliberate ceremony, not a routine save.

Concepts 14–15

Judgment calls

14How to Choose the Right CRDT Types

The same field can be modeled multiple valid ways with different concurrent outcomes: a Map string value is last-write-wins; a Text container merges everyone's edits; Lists can resurrect "deleted" elements; mergeable child containers converge where setContainer overwrites. "Choosing the right CRDT type means understanding their potential behavior in concurrent editing situations."

Classic

Schema design: pick the container whose merge behavior preserves invariants (Map for coordinates, not a 2-element List).

Conventional

The document defaults most apps want: Text CRDT for the body (merge prose), LWW values for title, URL, and settings (one winner is correct — nobody wants two titles interleaved).

Novel

Treat merge semantics as per-field editorial policy: Tree for a manuscript's binder so concurrent scene-moves stay a move, not a duplicate; MovableList for chapter order; LWW for "status: draft/final" where a merge would be meaningless. The concept reframes a technical menu as the product spec for what happens when a writer's two selves disagree.

15When Not to Rely on CRDTs

"CRDTs merge; they do not reject." No cross-replica locks or transactions, so exclusive resources, financial invariants, uniqueness, and authorization need a transactional authority or consensus — with CRDTs kept for the collaborative surface (text, lists, comments, drafts).

Classic

Don't build booking systems or ledgers on merge; both $60 withdrawals succeed locally and the merge overdrafts.

Conventional

In a writing product: prose merges via CRDT, while publish actions, permissions, billing, and share links stay server-authoritative.

Novel

The writing-specific reading: some editorial acts are exclusivity claims, not edits. "I'm restructuring chapter 2 tonight — hands off," a wiki slug that must be unique, "exactly one editor accepts this suggestion." A mature writing app pairs the merge layer with lightweight authority for those — soft locks as advisory presence, hard ones at the server — instead of pretending merge is agreement.

The arithmetic

From Loro's own Document Size benchmark (the Automerge-paper editing trace: 182,315 insertions + 77,463 deletions = 259,778 single-character operations, final document 104,852 characters). Derived columns re-computed independently; they match the source figures.

So: shallow is 76.8% smaller than the full snapshot raw (58.8% compressed) — inside the docs' "typically 70–90%" claim. Rule of thumb for a writing app, assuming roughly linear scaling with ops (assumption, not measured): keeping every keystroke of a document's life costs on the order of the document's own size once compressed — a 400 KB novel's complete history is megabytes, not gigabytes. Holes and opportunities, ranked by evidence

1 · Undo that survives closing, as a storage property

The strongest-evidenced gap: entire categories (system notes apps, all email drafts) ship zero history, and users notice. OpLog + DocState (04) means persistent, keystroke-grade history is the file format, with Changes (05) giving it human-sized entries. Evidence: strong — many products, documented complaints, benchmark economics.

2 · Non-destructive restore built on checkout + fork

Preview any version detached (03), restore by copy-forward or reverse-ops, never lose redo. Directly answers documented incumbent failures (destructive rollback, restore-destroys-redo). Evidence: strong — named product failures to fix.

3 · AI as a peer: provenance and one-gesture reject

PeerIDs (11) + transactions (06) + local undo (F4) make AI edits attributable, filterable, and rejectable as a unit. The attribution trend (iA, Linear) says the market is moving here. Evidence: medium-strong — trend documented, op-level implementation unshipped anywhere surveyed.

4 · Session versioning tuned to pauses, not timers

Change-merge windows (05) reproduce incumbents' hand-rolled intervals; writing-process research suggests pause-based boundaries would fit cognition better. Evidence: medium — intervals verified, pause-boundary benefit is inference from research literature.

5 · True forgetting: redaction and clean sharing

Shallow snapshots (13) enable erasure-into-history and process-free sharing that essentially no surveyed product offers. Demand is inferred from privacy norms and the Draftback discomfort, not from a complaint corpus. Evidence: medium — capability gap verified, demand less documented.

6 · Sync you can see

Version vectors (08) and import status (10) as UI: which device is behind, what's pending, over any transport. Aims at the conflicted-copy/draft-revert pain cluster. Evidence: medium — pain documented, UI treatment unproven.

7 · Selective / regional undo

Loro's UndoManager is linear and local; "undo just this paragraph's changes" (Azurite; Yu et al.'s CRDT selective undo) is not offered and remains a research-grade build on the OpLog. Evidence: exploratory.

Method and caveats

Pages were fetched and digested on 29 Aug 2026; digests are machine-summarized, then the load-bearing figures were re-fetched and quoted (undo defaults; the 70–90% and sync-boundary claims on the shallow-snapshot page; the full Document Size table) and the derived ratios recomputed in Python. The 1000-second Change-merge window is taken from the Operations-and-Changes digest and was not independently re-quoted — verify before relying on it. The behavioral grounding reuses the same-day incumbent survey rather than new fieldwork; items that survey marked unverified stay unverified. "Most writing is solo" is stated as a framing premise consistent with the surveyed tool population, not a measured statistic. Concept pages beyond the fifteen (tutorials, advanced, performance) were read only where cited.
