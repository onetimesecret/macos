Undo That Survives Closing

A second pass over how writing tools keep the past, extended to thirty-odd apps the first pass skipped, to the code editors, chat clients and design tools that solved neighbouring problems, and to the storage arithmetic that decides what is actually affordable.

29 Aug 2026 · builds on the existing "How the incumbents do versioning" notes · ~110 sources, spot-checked

Findings

The earlier notes sorted apps into four families and named four habits people bring with them. Both hold up. What this pass adds is that the family map was missing its most interesting members, that the loss people complain about most is one that per-device history cannot fix, and that the cost argument for rationing history is, on the numbers, not an argument at all.

01
Sync overwrite, not accidental deletion, is the dominant loss story.Across HN, Apple, Obsidian, Simplenote, Standard Notes, Samsung, Zoho, Outlook and Gmail threads, the largest cluster of complaints (about 13 distinct sources) is a note or draft silently reverting to an older copy from another device. It is noticed days later, often outside any 7-day retention window, and no "Recently Deleted" folder covers it.
02
Most history is device-local, which is exactly where it can't help.NotePlan's versions.db, UpNote's 50 versions per note per device, Standard Notes' free tier, Joplin's desktop-only viewer, VS Code and JetBrains local history: all live on one machine. The failure mode in 01 is a cross-device one.
03
Restore semantics are an unexamined design axis.Dropbox Paper deletes newer versions when you roll back. Figma writes two checkpoints so nothing is lost. Reflect and Joplin refuse to touch the current note and restore into a copy. Nobody in the earlier notes, and few vendors, say which they do. Users of AI tools now describe editing as "risky" for exactly this reason.
04
"When is a version?" has better answers than a five-minute timer.Novlr snapshots on pause. Paper requires 30 minutes and 500 changed characters. VS Code merges saves inside a 10-second window. Claude Code checkpoints per prompt. Writing-process research puts the cognitive pause threshold at about 2 s and shows pauses lengthen at sentence and paragraph boundaries; nobody ships a snapshotter that uses that.
05
Below-document restore exists, just not in note apps.JetBrains shows history for a selection. Google Sheets shows edit history for one cell. Roam keeps alternate versions of a block behind a sibling counter. Dabble's "Bring Forward" pulls one chapter out of a project snapshot. ChatGPT's < 2/3 > arrows are the smallest version UI in existence and users revolted when they were removed in 2026.
06
Storage is not the constraint. Ever, for short-form text.A heavy note-taker generating full-copy snapshots six times per session, twenty sessions a day, accrues 64 MB a year, about eighteen phone photos. A year of Notion-Business-tier retention costs roughly $0.0004 per user per month on S3. Plan gating of history is segmentation, not cost recovery.
07
Line diffs are the wrong tool for short prose, and worse than no compression.A note is often one long line, so unified line diffs measured at 0.5:1, larger than storing the full copy. Character deltas run about 1 byte per keystroke, the same order as a compacted CRDT log. Show word-level, store character-level.
08
Provenance is emerging as a layer orthogonal to time.iA Writer's Authorship annotations (per-grapheme ranges, SHA-256 checked) and Linear's July 2026 per-span author indicators answer "who wrote this?" rather than "what was this before?" Both are praised in principle and lightly adopted; the sidecar format is the complaint.

The "backups posing as history" family matters because its members are the scratchpad apps closest to the short-form use case. Restore semantics

The earlier notes treat "restore" as one verb. It is at least three, and the choice decides whether users feel safe pressing the button.

Destructive rollback truncates history to the restored point. Dropbox Paper says it plainly: "any more recent versions are deleted from version history." Cursor's checkpoint restore became this in 2025, and the forum thread is titled "Restore Checkpoint permanently destroys change history." Lightroom's History panel does it on every click into the past. It is the model users are most afraid of, and the one that makes people copy-all before trying anything.

Append makes restoring just another edit. Figma writes two checkpoints on restore, one for the state you left and one for the state you arrived at, so the timeline never loses a frame. Mem, macOS Versions, Stack Overflow rollbacks and Wikipedia reverts all work this way. Langfuse takes it furthest: versions are immutable and "current" is a movable label, so rollback copies nothing and deletes nothing.

Copy-out refuses to touch the live text. Reflect only offers "Restore to new note"; Joplin restores into a dedicated folder; Standard Notes and Canva offer it beside overwrite; macOS hides it behind Option-click. It is the most conservative and the most annoying, because it hands the user a merge job.

Design position
Append is the only one of the three that satisfies both habits from the first pass, "history as durable undo" and "snapshot before rewrite," without a modal. If the tool also shows the diff on the way back (below), copy-out becomes unnecessary: the user can see what they'd lose before they lose nothing.

One boundary case surfaced in Zed's tracker: after restoring an unsaved buffer on relaunch, pressing Undo wiped the buffer, because the restore itself was the last undoable action. Whatever the semantics, restore has to seal the undo stack.

What the keystroke-logging literature adds

Writing-process research (Inputlog, ScriptLog) has spent twenty years deciding where a writer's pauses fall. The conventional threshold for a cognitively meaningful pause is 2 seconds (Wengelin 2006; Hall, Baaijen and Galbraith 2022 note there is "no agreed threshold" but two seconds is typical). Van Waes and Leijten tested 200, 500, 1000 and 2000 ms and found the patterns robust. More useful for design: pauses get longer and more frequent as you move outward from within-word to between-word to sentence to paragraph boundaries, and between-sentence pauses correlate with looking back at larger text units, that is, with revision planning.

No tool surveyed uses this. The nearest is Novlr's "whenever you pause." A snapshotter that fires on idle ≥ 2 s with the caret at a sentence or paragraph boundary, with a fallback at idle ≥ 20 s anywhere and a hard trigger on paste, select-all-delete, AI rewrite or send, would produce versions that line up with the units writers already think in. That is what a person means by "the version before I rewrote that paragraph," and it is what the Time Machine plugin's "restore just a single paragraph" promise is reaching for from the other direction.

The cost of firing on every 2-second pause is real but bounded: roughly 60 versions per half-hour session instead of 6. The arithmetic section shows what that does to storage (it multiplies it by ten and it still fits in a few photos).

Below the document

The first pass ended on "all of them restore at whole-document granularity" and quoted the Time Machine plugin's paragraph-restore promise as the tell. The wider survey finds sub-document restore in five places, none of them note apps, and each one a different idea of what the unit is.

JetBrains' Local History has Show History for Selection: highlight a span, see only the revisions that touched it. Google Sheets' cell edit history (2019) does the same for one cell, with arrows to step through prior values. Roam's block versions keep alternates of one bullet behind a small counter and cycle them with Ctrl-< / Ctrl->. Dabble's Bring Forward lifts one chapter out of a project-wide snapshot into the present. Novelcrafter versions per field, so a scene's summary and its prose have separate timelines. macOS Versions and Word both fall back to the crude version: open the old copy beside the new one and paste across.

For short-form text the framing flips. When the whole item is a paragraph, "restore a paragraph" and "restore the document" are the same operation, and the interesting sub-unit becomes the sibling variant: the three phrasings of this prompt, the two tones of this reply. That is what ChatGPT's and Claude's < 2/3 > arrows are. OpenAI's community forum filled with threads in April, May and August 2026 when the arrows were removed or changed; one user wrote that "branching splits the conversation and makes it harder to compare versions quickly," and another that "I now worry that editing could permanently lose access to something I wanted to keep." The demand is for variants in place, not a tree.

The restore checkpoint/redo is too linear for my lizard brain. Am I wrong to want a tree-based agentic IDE?Hacker News, May 2025, on Cursor

Developers name the tree; everyone else asks for the arrows back. The undo-tree literature agrees in its silence: Vim and Emacs have shipped branching undo for two decades and there is no controlled usability study of it. The tree can exist underneath; the surface should be linear time plus in-place siblings.

Three things stand out. The number-one job is a multi-device job, and most history features are single-device. The number-two job is not history at all, it is the text surviving the moment it is usually lost (submit, close, crash), which is why the Zulip and hot-exit pattern of snapshotting on dismiss belongs in any short-form tool. And the AI-chat cluster is the youngest, angriest, and least served: three new OpenAI forum threads in four months, an XDA piece titled "Claude lost hours of my chat history overnight," and Cursor users discovering that restore now destroys redo.

Survey data on text loss specifically does not exist. Backblaze/Harris (2022, n=2,068) has 54% of US adults reporting lost data and 44% losing access when a shared or synced drive was deleted; Handy Recovery (2025, n=1,000) has 70.7%. Both are vendor-commissioned and neither asks about unsaved text.

The clipboard-manager finding deserves a line of its own. Across the HN threads, "select all, copy, then try the edit" is the universal folk workaround, described in 2008 as a "user-hostile workaround" and still in use. A tool that offers "Copy this version" from its history, or that simply reads the clipboard manager's own history as a fallback source, is meeting a habit that already exists.

The arithmetic

Two user profiles, chosen to be heavy rather than typical. A note-taker with 2,000 notes averaging 1.5 KB (a 2.9 MB corpus), editing 20 notes a day in half-hour sessions at one keystroke every 1.5 s, so 1,200 keystrokes per session, 24,000 a day, 8.76 million a year. And a temporary-text user writing 200 items a month at 800 bytes, each rewritten three times. One phone photo is taken as 3.5 MB.

The naive policy, full copies six times a session, is two photos a year for temporary text and eighteen for the heavy note-taker. It crosses 1 GB per year only when documents reach about 24 KB with six snapshots per session, or about 10 KB with pause-level snapshotting. That is long-form drafting territory, not notes, prompts or messages. Short text does not need a CRDT to afford history; it needs one only if you want concurrent editing or stable character identity for span-level restore, and then you pay 84 KB (Yjs) to 1.7–2.9 MB (Automerge, Loro) of runtime.

Retention and the gating question

Steady-state storage is daily accrual times retention. For the note-taker on the naive policy, accrual is 180 KB a day.

Notion charges roughly $10 a seat to move from 7 to 30 days of history; the marginal storage is $0.00009. Business, at about $15 more, buys 90 days for $0.0003. Even a pessimistic enterprise user (50 KB pages, forty sessions, three-way replication, history held in a hot database at $0.10/GB-month rather than object storage) tops out around $0.60 a month. History gating is a segmentation lever aimed at teams with compliance needs; the real costs are engineering ones: indexing, restore UI, and erasure obligations that must reach into every version. Loro's shallow snapshots and Yjs garbage collection exist for that last reason, not for bytes.

A second consequence: with tiered thinning (everything for an hour, hourly for a day, daily for a month, weekly for a year), a single note carries at most about 166 versions, and the list is still scrubbable. For an independent short-form tool, "unlimited history, on every device" is a differentiator that costs about a tenth of a cent per user per month.

Diffs for text that has no lines

On a 1.5 KB synthetic note with seven snapshots about 200 keystrokes apart, per-snapshot gzip ran 3.5:1, concatenated-snapshot compression 16:1 (synthetic vocabulary inflates this; expect 6–10:1 on real prose), and base-plus-character-deltas 4.9:1 at about 190 bytes per snapshot, roughly one byte per keystroke. Unified line diffs came out at 0.5:1: a note is often a single line, so the "changed line" is the whole note plus a header. Store character deltas or windowed compression; render at word level, which is what readers expect:

Thanks for the quickfast turnaround on this. I've attached the revised draftput the revised draft in the shared folder and flagged the two paragraphssections I'm least sure about.

The moved-paragraph problem that consumes Wikipedia's diff engineering (wikidiff2 detects moves by word-similarity between deleted and inserted paragraphs) mostly does not arise in text short enough to have no paragraphs to move. A sentence-level diff, jsdiff's diffSentences or equivalent, is a good summary unit: "2 sentences changed" beside a version is the prose equivalent of Wikipedia's byte delta.

Holes and opportunities

Where the tapestry is thin, in rough order of how much the evidence supports each.

History that lives with the note, not the device

Evidence: the largest complaint cluster is sync overwrite; most shipped history is device-local. Obsidian's 7-day default let a month-old overwrite go unnoticed.

Sync the history itself, and make retention longer than the noticing latency. Working-day retention (JetBrains) and tiered thinning together give a note a year of recovery points for a few hundred kilobytes. The version list should show which device wrote each version, because "the phone's copy won" is the question the user is asking.

Versions at cognitive boundaries

Evidence: keystroke-logging research puts natural pauses at ~2 s and at sentence and paragraph boundaries; no tool uses it; Novlr's "on pause" is the closest.

Fire on idle ≥ 2 s at a sentence or paragraph boundary, with a 20-second fallback, and hard triggers on paste, select-all-delete, AI rewrite, and send. Storage at 60 versions a session with delta encoding is still eighteen photos a year for the heaviest profile. This is the mechanism that makes "restore just this paragraph" fall out naturally, because the versions already sit at paragraph edges.

Variants in place, tree underneath

Evidence: users explicitly reject branching UIs as "harder to compare"; the sibling arrows are the most-mourned feature in OpenAI's forum this year; developers alone ask for trees.

Keep an undo tree in the model so nothing is ever unreachable, but surface it as linear time plus a < 2/3 > counter on any item that has alternates. Editing never hides the old branch; it adds a sibling. Restore appends; redo survives restore.

The draft's lifecycle ends at send

Evidence: no email client has draft history; Antinote's auto-expiry and iMessage's 15-minute edit window are the only tools that treat text as having a lifetime; "durable draft" is the second-largest job.

Temporary text has an event that ordinary notes lack: it gets sent, pasted, or submitted. Treat send as the canonical version and everything before it as drafts that thin out afterward. Snapshot on dismiss (Zulip's ≥3-character rule) so closing the box is never the moment of loss, and keep a "copy this version" affordance because the clipboard habit is not going away.

Provenance without the sidecar

Evidence: iA Writer Authorship is praised for the idea and criticised for a metadata block visible in every other editor; Linear shipped per-span author indicators in July 2026; "see what the AI changed" is a growing job.

Record who produced each span (self, AI, paste, reference) inside the history store rather than the file, and let the user revert one author's spans while keeping the rest. This is selective undo, which Azurite measured at about 2× faster than manual backtracking for code; the CRDT literature has selective undo for text since 2015 but nobody has shipped it to writers.

Diff-first history for text with no lines

Evidence: line diffs are worse than full copies on short prose; Standard Notes users asked for Meld; nobody shows a change-size signal in a version list.

Store character deltas, render word-level, summarise per sentence. Put "+14 / −9 words, 2 sentences" on each row of the list, the way Wikipedia puts byte deltas, so the user can find the big rewrite without opening anything.

Say what restore does

Evidence: the three restore semantics are undocumented almost everywhere and users of the destructive one describe editing as risky.

Append-only restore, two checkpoints per restore as Figma does, and a sealed undo stack afterward so Undo cannot "undo the restore." The control can then say what it does: "Restore" becomes "Bring this version forward," and nothing needs a confirmation dialog.

Muscle memory, extended

Additions to the four habits in the first pass, each one something hands already do somewhere.

Arrows on the block. Two years of ChatGPT and Claude have trained people to expect < 1/2 > beside anything that was edited, and to click them to compare. It is now the default expectation for "this text has other versions."

Tap "Edited." iMessage made the edited-label a control. Users of a short-form tool will tap it expecting to see the prior text.

Restore checkpoint. Cursor, Windsurf and Claude Code have taught developers to expect a checkpoint before every AI action and a one-click return. Anyone who has used an agentic editor will look for it before accepting an AI rewrite of prose.

Copy-all first. The oldest habit in the set. A tool can honour it (copy a version from history) rather than try to break it.

Ten minutes ago. Vim's :earlier 10m and Time Machine's "Enter Time Machine" both work in wall-clock time. People remember when, not which.

Method and caveats

Four parallel searches (writing apps, adjacent domains, technology and economics, user complaints) over roughly 110 web sources, August 2026, followed by spot-checks of the claims this report leans on hardest: Dropbox Paper's 30-minute/500-character thresholds and destructive rollback, Figma's 30-minute checkpoints and two-checkpoint restore, Standard Notes' plan prices, Linear's July 2026 attribution feature, and VS Code's local-history defaults (all confirmed against the vendor page). The storage figures were derived twice, independently. Reddit was unreachable, so the complaint corpus under-represents r/ObsidianMD, r/bearapp and r/ChatGPT. Byword, Bike, Paper, Capacities and Sudowrite's version behaviour could not be confirmed from documentation and are marked as such in the table.
